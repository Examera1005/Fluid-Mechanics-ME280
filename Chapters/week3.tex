\section{Week 3 - Hydrostatics: Variable Density \& Manometry}

Recall the fundamental equation of fluid statics: $\frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g$. For liquids, $\rho$ is constant. For gases, it varies.

\subsection{Ideal Gas Law \& Isothermal Atmosphere}

Using the Ideal Gas equation $P = \rho RT \implies \rho = \frac{P}{RT}$.

\begin{conceptbox}[Scientific Verification: Ideal Gas Law]
The formulation $P = \rho RT$ used in engineering was consolidated by Émile Clapeyron in 1834, combining Boyle's, Charles's, and Avogadro's empirical laws into a single rigorous equation of state.
\end{conceptbox}

Substituting this into the hydrostatic equation for an isothermal condition ($T = \text{cst}$):
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{P_{0}}^{P} \frac{dP}{P} = \int_{0}^{z} -\frac{g}{RT} \, dz
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
    \ln\left(\frac{P}{P_{0}}\right) = -\frac{g}{RT}z \implies P(z) = P_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{gz}{RT}\right)
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Manometry (Measuring Pressure)}

A manometer uses liquid columns to measure pressure differences. By stepping down and up the tube (pressure increases going down, decreases going up): 
\begin{equation*}
    P_{1} + \rho_{\text{fluid}}gz_{1} - \rho_{m}gh = P_{\text{atm}}
\end{equation*}

\begin{conceptbox}[Scientific Verification: The Manometer]
The concept of using fluid columns to measure differential pressure was invented by Christiaan Huygens around 1661. It directly relies on Stevin's and Pascal's principles that pressure in a continuous fluid only depends on depth.
\end{conceptbox}

\subsection{Forces on Submerged Surfaces}

\subsubsection{Flat Surfaces \& Center of Pressure}

Consider a flat surface of area $A$ inclined at an angle $\theta$. The pressure increases linearly with depth, creating a pressure prism.

\textbf{Resultant Force and Location}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Magnitude: $F_{R} = P_{c} \cdot A = (\rho gh_{c})A$
    \item Location (Center of Pressure): $y_{cp} = y_{c} + \frac{I_{xc}}{y_{c}A}$
\end{itemize}

Where $I_{xc}$ is the area moment of inertia. The Parallel Axis Theorem (Huygens, 1673) proves that $y_{cp} > y_{c}$, meaning the force always acts below the centroid due to the increasing pressure with depth.

\subsubsection{Curved Submerged Surfaces: Tank Sidewall Example}

For a curved surface, it is easier to calculate the horizontal and vertical components separately. Consider a tank with a curved quarter-circle sidewall of radius $R$ and width $w$.

\textbf{Vertical Component ($F_{V}$)}: Equals the weight of the fluid volume directly above the curved surface.
\begin{equation*}
    F_{V} = \int_{V} \rho g \, dV = \rho g \left( \underbrace{h \cdot R \cdot w}_{\text{Rectangle}} + \underbrace{\frac{\pi R^{2}}{4}w}_{\text{Quarter circle}} \right)
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Horizontal Component ($F_{H}$)}: Equals the force acting on the vertical projection of the curved surface (a rectangle of height $R$ and width $w$).
\begin{equation*}
    F_{H} = \int_{A_{v}} P \, dA = \rho g h_{\text{centroid}} A_{v} = \rho g \left(h + \frac{R}{2}\right)(R \cdot w)
\end{equation*}

\begin{conceptbox}[Scientific Verification: Archimedes' Principle]
The fundamental logic that the vertical force $F_{V}$ exerted by a fluid on a surface is equal to the weight of the fluid volume above it is rooted in the principles laid out by Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 250 BC) in his treatise \textit{On Floating Bodies}.
\end{conceptbox}

\subsection{Fluid Dynamics: The Bernoulli Equation}

\subsubsection{Derivation and Strict Assumptions}

Applying Newton's Second Law ($\Sigma\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$) along a streamline ($s$-direction):
\begin{equation*}
    -\frac{\partial P}{\partial s} - \rho g \frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = \rho v \frac{\partial v}{\partial s}
\end{equation*}

Integrating along the streamline with constant $\rho$ gives the Bernoulli Equation (1738):

\begin{eqbox}{Bernoulli's Equation}
\begin{equation}
    P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^{2} + \rho gz = \text{Constant}
\end{equation}
\end{eqbox}

\textbf{Strict Assumptions:} 1) Steady flow, 2) Incompressible fluid ($\rho = \text{cst}$), 3) Inviscid flow (no friction/shear), 4) Along a single streamline.

\subsubsection{Stagnation Point}

When fluid hits a blunt object, the streamline dividing the flow stops completely at the Stagnation Point (SP) where $v = 0$.

Using Bernoulli from point 1 to the Stagnation Point (point 2):
\begin{equation*}
    P_{1} + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{1}^{2} = P_{2} + 0 \implies P_{2} = P_{1} + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{1}^{2} = P_{\text{stagnation}}
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Flow Across Streamlines}

In the normal direction ($n$) towards the center of curvature $R_{n}$:
\begin{equation*}
    -\frac{\partial P}{\partial n} - \rho g \frac{\partial z}{\partial n} = \rho \frac{v^{2}}{R_{n}}
\end{equation*}

\begin{expertbox}[Normal Direction Integration]
Integrating this equation across the streamlines (along $dn$) yields the following exact result:
\begin{equation*}
    P + \rho gz + \rho \int \frac{v^{2}}{R_{n}} \, dn = \text{Constant}
\end{equation*}
This mathematically demonstrates that pressure increases moving radially outward away from the center of curvature.
\end{expertbox}
